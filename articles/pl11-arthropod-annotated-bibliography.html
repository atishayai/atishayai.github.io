PL11: Arthropod Annotated Bibliography

Paper #1:

Siemann, E., Tilman, D., Haarstad, J., &amp;amp; Ritchie, M. (1998). Experimental tests of the dependence of

arthropod diversity on plant diversity. The American Naturalist, 152(5), 738-750.

Siemann et al. (1998) conducted an experiment in a grassland in Minnesota to determine whether increasing plant diversity would lead to greater arthropod diversity. They changed species richness and sampled arthropods using sweep nets across 288 plots. They found that plots with more plant species supported more arthropod species instead of individual arthropods overall. Therefore, they concluded that the relationship between plant and arthropod diversity was weaker than expected as arthropod diversity was more closely linked to predator and parasite diversity. I plan to use this paper to support the community ecology section of the report to explore how plant richness can predict arthropod diversity in our own data.

Paper #2:

Brunet, J., Flick, A. J., &amp;amp; Bauer, A. A. (2021). Phenotypic selection on flower color and floral display

size by three bee species. Frontiers in Plant Science, 11, 587528.

Brunet et al. (2021) explored whether different bee species select differently on flower color and how the size of how flowers are displayed in alfalfa. After setting up 5 patches of alfalfa with 153 plants in Wisconsin, the experimenters used regression analyses after observing three bee species, namely: the honey bee, bumble bee, and leafcutting bee. They also observed flower color by analyzing the hue, chrome, and brightness along with the floral display size. They found that all three bee species selected for larger floral display sizes and flower color were selected less consistently than display size overall. Furthermore, only the leafcutting bees selected on flower hue/color and only bumble bees selected on flower chroma. I plan to use this paper in the sensory ecology section to demonstrate how different arthropod groups can produce varied responses to visual cues. This can also connect to the chi-square test we will conduct to compare Diptera and Hymenoptera color preferences.

Paper #3:

Leal, C. R. O., Oliveira Silva, J., Sousa-Souto, L., &amp;amp; de Siqueira Neves, F. (2016). Vegetation structure

determines insect herbivore diversity in seasonally dry tropical forests. Journal of Insect Conservation, 20(6), 979-988.

Leal et al. (2016) sought to determine if vegetation structure through tree richness and density determines insect herbivore density in seasonally dry tropical forests. They used foliage beating technique to sample herbivorous insects across 20 plots in three Brazilian ecoregions. They separated the insects they came across into leaf-chewing and sap-sucking to then use generalized linear models to test the effects of both tree richness and density on insect diversity. They concluded that tree richness positively increased both chewing and sap-sucking insect richness, but tree density only impacted sap-sucking insects. They then generalized this conclusion to determine that different feeding guilds respond differently to vegetation structure. I will use this paper in the community ecology section as it has a similar conclusion to the Siemann et al. (1998) paper, just in a tropic forest context. This is useful for discussing why arthropod groups in our data may show different responses to plant diversity as well.
