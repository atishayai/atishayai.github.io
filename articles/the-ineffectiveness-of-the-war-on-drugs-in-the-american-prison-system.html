The Ineffectiveness of the War on Drugs in the American Prison System

In a message to Congress on June 19, 1971, President Richard Nixon proposed the United States government’s highest level of concern as the prevalence of illicit drug use by stating, “If we cannot destroy the drug menace in America, then surely in time it will destroy us. I am not prepared 1 to accept this alternative.” Throughout his term, Nixon and his administration saw the waging drug epidemics of heroin, marijuana, and even cocaine by the time President Ronald Reagan had entered office in 1981. It was evident that the United States government had to act fast to take drugs off the streets and inevitably bring the epidemic to a hard stop. In Nixon’s presidency, the government’s response to the use of illicit drugs stemmed from “tough-on-crime” approaches to law enforcement which prioritized the incarceration of prisoners via mandatory minimum sentences and lengthier prison sentences. However, it was not until Reagan’s administration in 1982 that the term “War on 2 Drugs” was officially coined in response to the rapidly growing cocaine crisis in America. In an attempt to tackle the problem through the most effective means, from Nixon to Reagan and beyond, various legislations were passed by allocating federal spending and making drastic changes to the prison system as a whole, fueling the War on Drugs. Critics have debated the efficacy of the War on Drugs in terms of its original goals and intentions. Beginning in the 1970s into the 1980s, the War on Drugs was not only ineffective relative to their stated policy goals of eliminating drugs, but also exacerbated prisoners’ rights and racial inequality in the United States prison system due to the implementation of a “tough-on-crime” system, the disproportionate targeting of minority groups, and the increased government and private funding of prisons. The War on Drugs set up a precedent for years to come regarding how America took into account its drug problems. For instance, the War on Drugs has since informed the presidency of George W. Only through such efforts is it possible to begin to rectify the damage caused by the War on Drugs and work towards a fair and more equitable society for all.
